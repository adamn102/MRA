Batch analysis

Submit many sequences and compare them position by position, at the level of amino-acid change.

Read every difference on this page as a codon difference. The same fitted model is applied to every sequence you submit, so a batch does not measure many different mutation rates. Two sequences get different numbers at a position only when they carry a different codon there, or a different base immediately 3′ of it. Where they carry the same codon in the same context, their rates are identical to the last digit — not similar, identical. What a batch measures is therefore how the codon usage in a real set of viruses distributes mutational accessibility across the proteome.

The batch

Codons differ between sequences in two ways, and the distinction decides how a result should be read. A non-synonymous difference changes the residue, so it is visible in the protein and in the diversity measures below. A synonymous difference leaves the residue untouched — the sequences encode the same protein — yet still changes how readily that residue mutates, because the rate depends on the bases present, not on the amino acid they specify. Positions of the second kind look completely conserved at the protein level while differing in mutational supply, and they are usually the more interesting ones. Both are counted separately in the summary above.

What a set of circulating sequences can and cannot tell you

The fitted model is identical for every sequence you submit. A batch is therefore not a measurement of many different mutation rates — it is a measurement of how the codons actually present in your sequences distribute mutational supply. Four things follow from that, and one important limit.

  • Population-weighted supply. For any amino-acid change, the batch gives the observed distribution of wild-type codons and each codon's rate, so the weighted mean describes supply as it is distributed in the sampled population rather than in one reference codon. NL4-3 is a single draw; a few hundred sequences are a distribution.
  • Codon-usage sensitivity. At most positions every sample shares a codon and nothing varies. At the minority that are codon-polymorphic, the fold range says how much a virus's mutational options depend on which synonymous codon it happens to carry — and what fraction of the population sits on the high-supply version.
  • Supply against observed diversity. Mutability is a prediction about how readily a residue changes; diversity is an observation of how often it has. Positions that mutate readily yet never vary are where something removes the variants.
  • The accessible neighbourhood. Which residues a position can reach in one substitution, and how often each arises — the shape of what is available to selection, before selection acts.

The limit, stated plainly. Submitted sequences are related by descent, so observed diversity counts shared ancestry as well as independent mutation: two sequences agreeing at a position may reflect one ancestral state rather than two independent outcomes. Consensus sequences compound this, having already discarded within-host variation by construction. A set spanning several subtypes conflates between-subtype divergence with within-subtype polymorphism. And the model's position effects were fitted on NL4-3, so they transfer only as far as your sequences align to it. None of this prevents the comparison; it means the result describes the set you submitted, and is not an estimate of a selection coefficient.

What the shaded band and the intervals mean

Two different quantities appear on this page and they are easy to confuse, so they are kept apart deliberately.

  • The shaded band on the supply track is spread between your sequences, not uncertainty. It runs from the lowest to the highest rate observed across the samples covering each site. A wide band means those sequences carry different codons or different 3′ contexts there; a band that collapses onto the line means they agree. It is a min–max range rather than an interquartile range because the quartiles are identical at essentially every site — usually only one or two samples in a set differ at any given position, so the middle 50% carries no information here.
  • The 95% intervals on a rate are statistical uncertainty in the fitted model. They come from the ERR-Seq data the model was fitted to — how precisely that rate was measured — and they are a property of the atlas, identical for every sequence carrying the same codon and context. They are reported per amino-acid change on Browse the atlas and Your sequence.

The two can point in opposite directions: samples can agree exactly at a site whose rate is poorly determined, or differ widely at one measured precisely. A narrow band is not evidence that a rate is well estimated, and a wide band is not evidence that it is not. Nothing on this page carries per-sample error bars, because the model uncertainty does not vary between samples — only the codon does.
